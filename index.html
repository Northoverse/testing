EXHIBIT A — PLATE 01

The Architecture of the New Surveillance State

Leaked municipal smart-city contracts funnel resident data to private vendors — with no real consent.

A cache of contracts shows that data from "smart" sensors rarely stays with the city. In three jurisdictions the vendor — — retained a perpetual license to resident records even after the contract lapsed.

FILED 05.03.2026 · DESK AOPEN FILE
The pitch was seductive: sensors on every lamppost, traffic that flows like water, refuse collection routed by algorithm. What the brochures omitted was the back end. A cache of more than four thousand leaked municipal contracts, reviewed by The Vance over six months, shows that the data harvested by these "smart" systems rarely stays with the city that paid for it. Camera feeds, license-plate reads, transit taps and Wi-Fi pings are routinely funneled to private analytics vendors under terms residents were never shown. In three of the cities we examined the vendor retained a perpetual license to the data even after the contract ended; the privacy impact assessments, where they existed at all, were drafted by the vendors themselves. "Consent" was a banner on a website almost nobody reads. The result is a quiet, distributed surveillance apparatus assembled not by a spy agency but by procurement officers chasing efficiency grants.

Water Rights in the Age of Scarcity

At least nine funds have spent a combined acquiring senior Colorado River rights, betting that as the river shrinks the right to its water becomes the most valuable asset in the West.

FILED 04.28.2026OPEN FILE
For most of the twentieth century, water rights on the Colorado were the dull province of farmers, irrigation districts and state engineers. That is changing fast. The Vance has tracked at least nine investment funds that have spent a combined 1.2 billion dollars acquiring senior water rights across the basin since 2021, often by quietly buying the farmland those rights are attached to. The funds do not want to grow alfalfa. They are betting that as the river shrinks, the right to its water becomes one of the most valuable assets in the American West. Critics call it the financialization of thirst. When a hedge fund holds the most senior claim on a dwindling river, the question is no longer who farms the land, but who can afford to drink.

FILE // TECH POLICY

The Right to Repair the Future

Internal engineering memos show how █████████████ use software locks to route every repair through their own networks, turning fixable hardware into disposable e-waste by design.

FILED 04.21.2026OPEN FILE
A tractor that refuses to start until a licensed technician types in a code. A phone that disables its own camera when a third party swaps the glass. A ventilator that a hospital is contractually forbidden from fixing. These are not bugs; they are the business model. Through internal engineering memos and dealer agreements, The Vance documents how a handful of manufacturers have engineered repair out of their products and routed every fix back through their own service networks. The environmental toll is enormous: tens of millions of devices discarded not because they are broken, but because they are locked. A growing coalition of farmers, nurses and tinkerers is now fighting back in statehouses across the country, and the manufacturers are spending heavily to stop them.

FILE // JUSTICE

Algorithms in the Courtroom

Scoring data for reveals opaque risk-assessment tools leaning on proxies for poverty and geography to set bail — formulas guarded as trade secrets.

FILED 04.14.2026OPEN FILE
In dozens of jurisdictions the decision to hold a defendant in jail before trial is now shaped by a number generated by software. The tools promise objectivity: feed in a person's history, receive a "risk score," let the judge decide. The Vance obtained scoring data for more than thirty thousand cases and found the promise hollow. The models lean heavily on proxies for poverty and geography, penalizing defendants for where they live and who they know. Two people accused of the same offense can receive wildly different scores. The vendors guard their formulas as trade secrets, so neither the accused nor their lawyers can examine how the number was reached. A risk score, it turns out, is only as fair as the system that trained it — and that system was never fair to begin with.
CASE No. 047RESTRICTED
FILE // ENVIRONMENT

The Ghost Flights of Europe

Flight-tracking data exposes near-empty jets flown across the continent each winter — carriers burning carbon to protect takeoff slots under a use-it-or-lose-it rule.

FILED 04.05.2026OPEN FILE
Under a use-it-or-lose-it rule, airlines must operate a minimum share of their scheduled flights or forfeit the takeoff and landing slots that are their most valuable possessions at congested airports. So when demand collapses, carriers face a perverse choice: fly nearly empty aircraft, or surrender slots worth tens of millions. The Vance analyzed flight-tracking data across a single winter season and identified thousands of these "ghost flights," jets crossing the continent with a handful of passengers or none at all. The carbon released to protect a spreadsheet entry runs to hundreds of thousands of tonnes. Regulators have known about the loophole for years. Closing it, it turns out, means taking on the most powerful lobby in aviation.
